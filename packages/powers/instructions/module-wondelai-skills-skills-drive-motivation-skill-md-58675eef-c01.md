# Support autonomy over task, time, technique and team

Autonomy is the desire to direct one's own life through choice over what, when, how, and with whom; it is not independence, since people can choose while interdependent with a team. In product design, enable choice through task, time, technique, and team; examples include selectable onboarding paths, user-controlled feeds, and customizable workflows. Forced prerequisites, unskippable tutorials, mandatory notifications, and single forced paths violate autonomy. When designing onboarding, feeds, or workflow controls, consult references/autonomy.md for Four T's patterns and the audit checklist.

# Avoid the listed motivation mistakes

Avoid awarding points for everything; reserve rewards for meaningful milestones. Make participation opt-in. Match challenge to individuals with adaptive difficulty. Make progress visible through progress indicators or skill tracking. Connect every feature to its purpose. For if-then bonuses, pay fairly, use now-that rewards, and focus on autonomy, mastery, and purpose.

# Run the motivation diagnostic

Audit any motivation system with these six questions and actions for a no answer: can users choose what, when, and how (if no, add choices, flexibility, and customization); can users see progress (if no, add progress tracking and skill levels); is challenge matched to skill (if no, implement adaptive difficulty); is there immediate feedback (if no, add real-time response to actions); does the user know why the system matters (if no, connect to mission and show impact); are if-then rewards used (if no, switch to now-that rewards or intrinsic design).

# Design ethical gamification

For gamification, prefer opt-in, chosen challenges over forced participation; skill-based progression and meaningful milestones over points for everything and trivial badges; and community contribution or personal growth over pointless competition or discouraging leaderboards. The Duolingo example combines choice of language, pace, and topics; adaptive difficulty and skill levels; and a purpose of connecting with people. Streaks can shift motivation from intrinsic mastery to extrinsic loss aversion.

# Avoid if-then rewards for creative work

If-then rewards can extinguish intrinsic motivation, diminish performance, crush creativity, crowd out good behavior, encourage cheating, become addictive, and foster short-term thinking. Extrinsic rewards work only for routine, algorithmic tasks with no intrinsic interest; they backfire for creative work, complex problem-solving, or long-term engagement. When a reward or incentive scheme is backfiring, consult references/extrinsic-rewards.md for the named studies and the decision rule for safe reward use.

# Prefer intrinsic motivation for cognitive work

For any task requiring even rudimentary cognitive effort, external rewards either do not work or actively worsen performance. Intrinsic motivation through autonomy, mastery, and purpose drives lasting engagement.

# Design a mastery path

Mastery is the desire to improve at something that matters and is an asymptotic pursuit, not a destination. Treat ability as developable and frame failure as learning rather than judgment; mastery requires effort and deliberate practice. Calibrate challenge to skill because flow lies between boredom and anxiety; support flow with clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge-skill balance, and a sense of control. Offer a next level or challenge, show skill development, adapt difficulty, and provide clear feedback. Flat, non-adaptive difficulty and punishing failure violate mastery. When designing progress, difficulty, or feedback, consult references/mastery.md for flow calibration, deliberate practice, and the audit checklist.

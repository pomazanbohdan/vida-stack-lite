# Run management as an output system

A manager's output is their organization's output plus that of neighboring organizations under their influence. Emails, meetings, reviews and decisions count only insofar as they raise combined output. Since managerial time is scarce, continually choose the activity creating the most output per hour and eliminate negative-leverage activities.

# Avoid the listed management mistakes

Do not measure activity instead of output; count shipped work that survives and pair quantity with quality before publishing indicators. When busy, reschedule 1:1s but never drop them. Avoid rank-based decisions; discuss freely and let the named decider decide. Keep OKRs as a stretch tool rather than a compensation formula. Match management style to TRM task by task. Inspect specifications and plans before production so defects are caught at a lower-cost stage. Deliver feedback when events happen rather than saving it for an annual review.

# Make decisions with explicit roles and stretch OKRs

Make decisions through free discussion with dissent welcome, a crisp stated decision (crisper when more contentious), and full support after disagreement. Decide at the lowest competent level closest to current technical knowledge. Planning assesses environmental demand, faces current status honestly, and closes the gap through decisions and actions now rather than documents; plan for next year's gap rather than this week's firefighting. Break peer-group indecision with a peer-plus-one: a sanctioned senior participant who can tip the decision. Before every decision meeting, specify what decision is needed, by when, who decides, who is consulted, who ratifies or vetoes, and who is informed. If no one person has both needed forms of authority, pair the freshest technical knowledge with the strongest organizational judgment. Reverse decisions openly when new facts arise; quiet reversals are waffling. Use objectives to state where to go and measurable key results to track progress; keep objectives few, make results scorable without argument, cascade each level's key results into the next level's objectives, and never mechanically tie OKRs to compensation.

# Run the management diagnostic

Use the diagnostic questions to check whether team output can be stated in one sentence; every quantity metric has a quality pair; the limiting step is known; reports set their 1:1 agendas; 1:1 frequency follows TRM (weekly for new-to-task, monthly for veterans); major decisions sit at the lowest competent level; OKRs would remain the same without pay attached; and the manager personally taught the team something this quarter. For each negative answer, perform the listed corrective action, including defining output and building 4–6 indicators around it, pairing measures, finding and scheduling around the queue bottleneck, returning agenda ownership to reports, naming decision roles, decoupling OKRs from compensation, and scheduling manager-taught training.

# Choose work by managerial leverage

Managerial leverage is output per unit of managerial time. Favor work affecting many people or redirecting substantial work, such as training, prepared decisions and information gathering. Avoid negative leverage from meddling with experts, delaying decisions and visible gloom. Delegate tasks known best by the manager; delegation without monitoring is abdication. Monitor tasks rather than people, sampling deeper at low task-relevant maturity and lighter as it rises. Schedule 1:1s, staff meetings, reviews and planning first, fitting interrupts around them. Operate below full load so surprises do not delay downstream work; decline work early when full. Batch interruptions with office hours or checkpoints. Do not hoard information or approvals into a bottleneck.

# Run process meetings and 1:1s

Use regular process meetings, including 1:1s, staff meetings, and operation reviews, to carry most managerial work, at roughly one quarter of the calendar; mission-oriented meetings are ad hoc and exist to produce a decision. If ad hoc mission meetings consume more than about one quarter of managerial time, treat the process as broken. The subordinate owns and brings the 1:1 agenda; the supervisor listens, takes notes of agreed actions, and asks what else is on the subordinate's mind after the agenda. Set 1:1 frequency by task-relevant maturity, not seniority or affection: weekly for new-to-task, every few weeks for veterans, and never less often than monthly. Both parties keep a hold list for non-urgent topics. Run staff meetings as controlled free discussion with the manager moderating as a Socratic prompter; recurring ad hoc meetings are process meetings in denial. Do not turn 1:1s into status extraction; put status in writing.

# Measure output indicators, not activity

Measure shipped output that survives, not activity such as code lines, hours or tickets touched. Pair every quantity indicator, including administrative throughput, with a quality counterpart so neither can be optimized at the other's expense. Favor leading indicators that warn early enough to act; compare trend indicators with the team's history and forecast. Re-forecast the same horizon each period in a stagger chart; reading down a column reveals honest, optimistic or sandbagged forecasts. Indicators measure work, not workers; surveillance encourages optimizing numbers over output.

# Manage the team as a production line

Treat every team as a production line, even when flow is not visible. Make flow visible and organize around the limiting step, scheduling other work around the longest, hardest or most expensive stage. Fix defects at the lowest-value stage before downstream work raises their cost. Batch work when setup costs are high. Most knowledge work is built to forecast rather than order: staff to the forecast and accept controlled risk. Since not all work can be watched, monitor it as a black box through a handful of indicators. Optimize systems, never treat people as interchangeable machines.

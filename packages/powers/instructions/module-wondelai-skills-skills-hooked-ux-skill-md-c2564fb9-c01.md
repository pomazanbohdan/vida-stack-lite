# Make the action dead simple

The action is the simplest behavior performed in anticipation of a reward, guided by the Fogg model: behavior occurs when motivation, ability, and trigger converge. Reducing friction by increasing ability is more reliable than trying to increase motivation; each extra step, field, or decision creates a drop-off point. Assess simplicity across time, money, physical effort, brain cycles, social deviance, and non-routine; simplify the anticipated behavior rather than the full task, and reduce choices because more choices slow decisions. When simplifying, do not hide cost or consequence: a one-tap purchase must still show its price and commitment.

# Apply the Hook Model to habit-forming products

Use the Hook Model as a framework for building habit-forming products through successive cycles of a four-phase loop that connects a user problem to a solution frequently enough to shift use from deliberate to automatic; habits are built, not created.

# Avoid the listed Hook Model mistakes

Avoid relying indefinitely on external triggers; map the product to an emotion and transition to internal triggers within 30 days. Avoid an overly complex core action; simplify it to the minimum viable action and apply the six ability factors. Avoid predictable rewards; add variability across Tribe, Hunt, and Self rewards. Ask for investment only after users receive reward, sequencing Trigger → Action → Reward → Investment. Do not ignore hook ethics; use the Manipulation Matrix and be a Facilitator, not a Dealer.

# Run the Hook Model diagnostic

For any product feature, diagnose whether it has an internal trigger, a dead-simple action, a variable reward, and an investment that loads the next trigger. If the internal trigger is missing, research user emotions; if the action is not simple, remove friction; if the reward is not variable, add unpredictability; if investment does not load the next trigger, connect investment to triggers.

# Test whether a habit formed

A habit is considered formed when at least 5% of users show unprompted, habitual usage. Test by identifying which users engage most frequently and what they share; identifying the Habit Path action sequence distinguishing power users from casual users; and finding the internal trigger and emotion preceding use.

# Assess the Habit Zone

A product can enter the Habit Zone when it combines high perceived value with high frequency of engagement. High perceived value at low frequency is viable but needs advertising or marketing; low perceived value is failure at either frequency. Assess how often users need to engage, the perceived value of each engagement, and whether frequency is high enough for automatic behavior.

# Design the investment

Investment is a user contribution—time, data, effort, social capital, or money—that improves the product for the next use, raises switching costs, and loads the next trigger. It should follow reward rather than precede it, because it improves the next cycle rather than providing immediate reward. Each investment should load the next trigger, and small investments can compound into stored value and better personalization. Investment must genuinely improve the experience; do not trap users with artificial switching costs or impossible data export. Make staying the better choice through real value.

# Connect the problem to a solution through the four-phase loop

Connect a user's problem to a solution through a four-phase loop often enough to form a habit, moving usage from deliberate to automatic: Trigger → Action → Variable Reward → Investment, then back to Trigger.

# Expand to adjacent segments after dominance

After dominating the beachhead, expand to adjacent segments whose needs allow whole-product transfer, whose buyers trust beachhead references, and whose entry requires incremental effort. Do not jump to distant segments before beachhead dominance.

# Place buyers on the adoption lifecycle

The adoption lifecycle runs from innovators through early adopters, a chasm, early majority, late majority, and laggards. Pragmatists need proven, working solutions, peer references, present value, and low risk, unlike visionaries. Do not market to both groups simultaneously; the reference catch-22 is why crossing requires deliberately creating early-majority proof.

# Address one buyer group at a time

Distinguish five buyer groups by needs: innovators want new technology, early adopters seek vision and advantage, early majority need a complete and de-risked solution plus peer references, late majority want supported commodities, and laggards adopt only when forced. Do not market to visionaries and pragmatists simultaneously; pragmatists’ reference requirement creates a catch-22 addressed by the next steps.

# Avoid common chasm-crossing mistakes

Avoid selling to early-majority buyers like early adopters, pursuing multiple beachheads, leaving the whole product incomplete, using revolutionary positioning, or skipping references; instead build the whole product, choose one segment, frame proven value, and invest in references.

# Match go-to-market to the buyer

Execute go-to-market in a way that matches the buyer: founder-led direct sales for early adopters, proof-rich partner/channel approaches for the early majority, and lower-touch approaches for the late majority. For mainstream crossing, lead with references, whole-product completeness, ease and low risk; frame the offer as proven evolution, demonstrate ROI, and use channels pragmatists trust.

# Position for a target customer and need

Position for a target customer and need by naming the product category, key benefit, primary alternative, and primary differentiation. The alternative may be a manual process, spreadsheet, or legacy system rather than a direct competitor. Differentiate on a dimension you dominate and make the incumbent’s strength irrelevant.
